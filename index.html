Cirque Lake

A lake with no trailhead, hanging in a granite bowl above Callaghan Lake. You paddle to reach the climb, and you climb to reach the water. An expedition in two acts.

The Premise

There is no trail around Callaghan Lake. That single fact is the doorman for everything that follows. Cirque Lake hangs in a granite bowl above the far southwest shore, and the only way to reach the climb is to cross the water first — roughly two kilometres of open lake between the launch and the inlet where the route begins. No boat, no trip. In a corridor where most alpine water now comes with a parking problem, that filter is the whole gift.

The Callaghan is the quiet arm of the valley system — up the Callaghan Valley road off Highway 99 between Squamish and Whistler, past the Nordic venue turnoffs, until pavement gives up and a rough gravel road carries you the last stretch to Callaghan Lake Provincial Park. Something with clearance, ideally all-wheel drive, earns its keep here. For paddlers who like their water sports with a vertical component, what waits at the end of that road is arguably the best value in the corridor: a flat-water crossing, a fierce little climb, and a lake most visitors to the valley never learn exists.

We’ve structured this guide the way the day actually unfolds — as an expedition in two acts. The paddle is Act I. The climb is Act II. The reward gets its own billing.

“Cirque Lake doesn’t have a trailhead. It has a shoreline you have to earn twice — once with your arms, once with your legs.”

Two kilometres of water stand between you and the climb.

From the launch at Callaghan Lake, aim across to the far southwest shore — the inlet area where the outflow creek from Cirque Lake comes down. On a still morning it’s a gorgeous, easy paddle: granite walls on both sides, subalpine fir down to the waterline, the bowl you’re headed for visible high on the skyline if you know where to look.

The catch is the wind. Callaghan Lake sits in a funnel, and on warm days the afternoon wind pours down the valley and can turn the return crossing into genuinely hard work — whitecaps-and-grim-faces hard, in a loaded canoe with tired arms. The move is the oldest one in the book: launch early, cross in the morning calm, and hug the shoreline rather than committing to the middle of the lake. Wear the PFDs the whole way; this is cold alpine water a long way from help.

The boat problem, solved

No boat? This trip is the reason canoe delivery exists. Squamish Canoe Rental rents proper three-seat tripping canoes with paddles and PFDs included, and you can book online through Rented Local — ask about delivery options for the Callaghan when you book, and mention the rough final road so the logistics get sorted in advance. Prefer to haul your own gear? Pickup and dropoff at their Squamish base works too. Three paddlers per canoe is the sensible maximum, and for this trip two is even better: lighter boat, easier carry up the beach, faster crossing. The same crew runs Squamish Water Taxi — jet-boat crossings on the Squamish River and Howe Sound tours — when a trip needs the far side of bigger water.

At the far shore, land near the inlet, haul the canoe well up out of the water, and flip it. You’ll be gone for hours, and lake levels and weather both change. Take a good look back at the launch side before you walk away — you want to recognize your line home when the light is different.

Short, steep, and honest — the creek is your handrail.

From the landing, the route climbs beside the outflow creek that drains Cirque Lake — the same white thread you can see from the water. Call it a kilometre and a half to two kilometres, gaining somewhere in the neighbourhood of 250 to 350 metres. On paper that sounds like a warm-up. Underfoot it’s a proper little fight: steep, rooty, rough in places, with sections where hands come out of pockets and stay out.

Be clear-eyed about what this path is. It’s an unofficial, unmaintained route worn in by the people who’ve gone before you, not a built and signed trail — expect it to be faint in places, expect deadfall, and expect conditions to vary year to year. Sturdy shoes with real tread are the difference between a fun scramble and a miserable one, especially on the way down when the roots are damp. If the route ever feels wrong, the creek is your handrail: the way up stays close to the water.

Company in the valley

This is bear country, full stop. Guides who work this valley report both bear and bobcat sightings around Callaghan Lake, and the climb follows exactly the kind of noisy creek corridor where you and a bear can surprise each other. Make noise, carry bear spray where you can reach it, keep food packed away, and give any wildlife the whole right-of-way.

The climb ends the way all the best ones do: abruptly. The angle relents, the trees thin, granite opens up in front of you — and the bowl is suddenly there.

The Reward

A granite amphitheatre with a lake for a stage.

i.

Slabs that slide into the water

The shoreline here isn’t beach — it’s clean granite slab dipping straight into clear alpine water. Warm rock, cold lake, nowhere to be. This is the lunch spot the whole day was for.

ii.

The amphitheatre walls

Big cirque walls wrap the far side of the bowl and hold the acoustics of the place — talk quietly and the lake talks back. Late-lying snow patches feed the water well into summer.

iii.

Ledges people scout first

There are cliff-jumping ledges here, and people do use them — after swimming the landing zone first, checking depth and underwater rock, every single time. Alpine lakes hide boulders in dark water. Scout carefully or spectate happily; both are good days.

iv.

A fraction of the crowds

The double filter — rough road, then mandatory paddle — means Cirque usually sees a fraction of the people the drive-up lakes get. On a weekday you have decent odds of an empty bowl.

Leave it as found

There are no facilities anywhere past the Callaghan Lake launch — no outhouses, no bins, no water taps, nothing. Everything you carry in comes back out, including the stuff that feels biodegradable. The alpine shoreline here is fragile and slow-growing; stay on rock where you can, keep soap out of the lake, and leave the bowl exactly as quiet as you found it.

The Almanac

A late-melting road sets the calendar.

The gravel road to Callaghan Lake sits high and holds snow late — most years the realistic window is July through October, but it varies. Check current road and conditions reports (and BC Parks notices for Callaghan Lake Provincial Park) before you commit to the drive.

July The window usually creaks open. Snow patches linger in the bowl, the waterfall runs loud, mosquitoes collect their toll. Confirm the road has melted out before driving up. Go, with checks

Honest Note

What the brochures leave out.

This is a remote trip dressed up as a short one. The numbers look modest — a couple of kilometres of paddling, a short climb — but you’re combining open alpine water, an unmaintained route, and a valley with no services, no cell reliability worth betting on, and help a long way off. The wind is the part people underestimate. A crossing that took forty easy minutes in the morning can be a genuine struggle at four in the afternoon. Build your day around the return paddle, not the departure.

The road will also be worse than you’re picturing — rough gravel that’s hard on low cars. And the climb, though short, is steeper and rootier than its distance suggests; this is not the day for running shoes with dead tread or for paddlers who’d rather not use their hands.

None of this is a reason to skip it. It’s the reason the bowl is still quiet. Tell someone your plan, wear the PFDs, launch early, and Cirque gives you one of the finest days in the corridor.

Questions & Answers

What people ask before they go.

Can you hike to Cirque Lake without a canoe?

No — and this is the defining fact of the trip. There’s no trail around Callaghan Lake, so the only practical way to reach the climb is to paddle roughly 2 km across to the far southwest shore. The paddle is mandatory. If you don’t own a boat, Squamish Canoe Rental rents canoes with paddles and PFDs included — book through Rented Local and sort delivery when you reserve, or pick up and drop off at their Squamish base yourself.

How hard is the climb from Callaghan Lake to Cirque Lake?

Short but legitimately steep. Figure roughly 1.5–2 km following the outflow creek, gaining on the order of 250–350 m, on an unofficial, unmaintained route that’s rooty and rough in places. Most reasonably fit hikers manage it fine with sturdy shoes and unhurried pacing — just don’t expect a built trail, and expect to use your hands here and there.

What’s the road in like?

The Callaghan Valley road leaves Highway 99 between Squamish and Whistler; the final stretch to Callaghan Lake Provincial Park is rough gravel. All-wheel drive and decent clearance are recommended — low sedans have a bad time. The road also melts out late; check current conditions before driving up, especially early in the season.

When is the best time to go?

Realistically July through October, with August to early September the sweet spot — warmest water, longest days, most settled weather. Whatever the month, launch early: afternoon winds funnel down the valley and can make the return crossing real work.

Is the cliff jumping safe?

Only as safe as your scouting. There are ledges people jump from, but alpine lakes hide boulders in dark, cold water, and levels change through the season. The people who do this well swim the landing zone first and check depth every visit — never trust last year’s memory or someone else’s word. If you can’t verify the depth, don’t jump. Rescue here is hours away at best.

Are there bears in the Callaghan?

Yes — this valley is active bear country, and guides who work it report both bear and bobcat sightings around Callaghan Lake. Travel in a group, make noise on the climb (the creek masks your sound), carry bear spray somewhere reachable, and keep food sealed and packed. Wildlife encounters here overwhelmingly end fine when people give animals space and no reason to linger.

Are there facilities? Can I camp?

Past the Callaghan Lake launch there are no facilities at all — no outhouses, bins, or water. Pack out absolutely everything, and treat the fragile alpine shoreline gently. For camping at Callaghan Lake itself or anywhere in the park, check BC Parks’ current information for Callaghan Lake Provincial Park before you go — rules and site status change, and we’d rather point you at the source than guess.
